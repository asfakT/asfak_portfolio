How a news feed shows you your friends' posts

By Asfak Shahrier

Track System designLevel Case studyStatus GrowingAbout 13 min read

I open Facebook, scroll for a minute, and close it. It feels like the simplest screen on the internet: a list of posts from people I follow, newest first. But the same list has to be built for about two billion people, some of whom follow thousands of accounts, while a few accounts have tens of millions of followers. These notes rebuild that feed step by step, and the whole story turns out to be about one question: do you do the work when someone posts, or when someone reads?

Before we start

How this case study is built

Five versions, each fixing what the last one couldn't handle.

v1Save posts and follows in DynamoDB
v2Build the feed at read time by querying everyone I follow
v3Precompute each feed when a post is created
v4Queue + workers, and skip huge accounts (hybrid)
v5Replicated post cache for viral posts
Why this order

WhatsApp was about delivering one message to the right people. LeetCode was about running strangers' code. A news feed is about reads: people scroll far more often than they post. So every version below moves a bit more work away from the moment you open the app, until reading your feed is mostly one lookup.

Step 1

What the system must do

Functional

  • Create a post
  • Follow other users (one direction, like Twitter)
  • See posts from people I follow, newest first
  • Keep scrolling (page through the feed)

Non-functional

  • Availability over consistency: a post may take up to 1 minute to show up
  • Posting and loading the feed in under 500 ms
  • About 2 billion users
  • No limit on follows or followers

Left out on purpose

  • Likes and comments
  • Private or friends-only posts
  • Unfollowing
  • Login (assume a session or JWT)

Why it works this way

Follow uses PUT, not POST, because following someone twice should change nothing. That's what idempotent means: repeat the call and the result is the same. The Follow table answers three questions with plain key lookups: "does A follow B?" (point read), "who does A follow?" (range on the main key), and "who follows A?" (range on the swapped-key GSI, a global secondary index, which is DynamoDB's word for a second way to look up the same rows). Nothing needs friends-of-friends, so no graph database is needed.

Step 3

The full architecture at a glance

The map we're building toward. Each later section zooms into one part of it.

Why it works this way

Everything sits in DynamoDB because every access is by a known key: a post by ID, a user's follows by user ID, a feed by user ID. DynamoDB gives full throughput only when keys are spread evenly across partitions, which is true for most of this data and false for one viral post. That exception gets its own section below, just like the hot-video partitions in my YouTube note.

Step 4

What happens when you post, and when you scroll

Two flows, two very different shapes. Posting returns early and leaves work for later. Reading gathers a few pieces and stitches them together.

Why it works this way

Posting is a long-running task hidden behind a quick reply, the same trick as LeetCode's "Pending" and YouTube's video processing: accept, return an ID, finish in the background. The 1-minute staleness allowance in the requirements is what makes this legal. If the product demanded "every follower sees it instantly", this design would not work.

Deep dive 1

When you follow thousands of accounts

Version 2 built the feed at read time: look up everyone I follow, fetch each one's recent posts, merge, sort. Fine for 50 follows. Painful for 5,000.

BAD

Fan-out on read only

One feed load turns into thousands of queries, then a big merge. The rare heavy users are exactly the ones who blow the 500 ms target.

GREAT

Fan-out on write (precomputed feed)

When someone posts, push the post ID into every follower's feed row. Reading becomes one key lookup. Chosen (with a twist in the next section).

Why it works this way

The cap of ~200 IDs is a product choice as much as a technical one. Almost nobody scrolls past 200 posts, so deep paging can fall back to the slow read-time query. Product limits help too: Facebook caps friends at 5,000. Twitter made the same choice at its own scale: its timeline cache kept at most 800 entries per home timeline in Redis, each copy replicated on 3 machines, and only for users active in the last 30 days, so inactive accounts cost no memory at all.

Deep dive 2

When one account has 90 million followers

Fan-out on write has a dark side. One post from a celebrity means tens of millions of feed writes. This is the "celebrity problem".

BAD

Post service writes every feed itself

Millions of writes inside one request: connection limits, a timeout long before 500 ms, and one unlucky host doing all the work.

GOOD

Queue + async feed workers

Put postId and creatorId on SQS (at-least-once delivery) and let workers fan out. But a 1M-follower job is 1,000× a 1K one, so big jobs need splitting.

GREAT

Hybrid feed

Flag huge accounts as "not precomputed" on the Follow table. Workers skip them; the feed service pulls their recent posts at read time and merges. Chosen.

Why it works this way

This is the same idea as WhatsApp's large groups: past a certain size, sending one copy per member stops making sense, so you switch strategy. Twitter hit this in real life. Its write fan-out reached 1 million followers in about 3.5 seconds at the median, but a tweet from an account with 31 million followers could take up to 5 minutes to land everywhere. Their fix was the same hybrid: merge those accounts in at read time.

Deep dive 3

When one post goes viral

Feeds store only IDs, so every feed load fetches post content by ID. A viral post is fetched by millions of feeds in its first hours, and all those reads hit the same key.

BAD

Read straight from DynamoDB

DynamoDB likes evenly spread keys. One post at hundreds of reads per second (say 500 rps) overloads its partition.

GOOD

Sharded Redis cache

Cache posts by ID with a long TTL and LRU eviction (posts rarely change). But sharding by ID puts the viral post on one shard: the hot key moved, it didn't vanish.

GREAT

Replicated cache

N independent cache copies, each able to hold any post, behind a load balancer. A hot post is served by all N. Chosen.

Why it works this way

The trade-off is honest: replication wastes memory on duplicates and multiplies cold misses, but DynamoDB can absorb a few extra misses. In my YouTube note the hot thing was a popular video's metadata; here it's a post. Same lesson both times: sharding spreads different keys, replication spreads one key. Facebook's real system, TAO, pushes this further with layers of cache tiers: follower caches in each region that serve reads and forward misses to a leader tier, with replication lag normally under a second.

Deep dive 4

Scrolling without page numbers

A feed changes while you read it. New posts arrive at the top every few seconds, so "page 2" means something different a moment later.

OptionVerdictWhy
Offset (?page=2)BADNew posts shift everything down, so you see repeats; the database also has to skip over earlier rows
Timestamp cursorGREATStable while the feed changes, and maps straight onto the creatorId + createdAt GSI. Chosen.
Why it works this way

For a precomputed feed the cursor just says where to continue in the list of ~200 IDs. Past the end of that list, the feed service falls back to the slow read-time query with the same cursor. Few people ever scroll that far, which is why the fallback can be slow.

Reflection

What surprised me

The feed is just IDs

I pictured each feed as a copy of posts. It's a short list of ~200 IDs, about 2 KB. The content lives once, in the Post table and the cache.

No single right answer

Push vs pull isn't a choice you make once. The best design does both, and the dial is set per account based on follower count.

Staleness is a feature

That 1-minute allowance quietly enables the queue, the workers and the cache. Strict "instant" delivery would make every part harder.

Practice

Try it yourself

Think each one through (or explain it to a friend), then open the answer.

Why is building the feed at read time a problem?

Someone who follows thousands of accounts triggers thousands of queries plus a big merge on every load. Those requests are rare but impossible to serve under 500 ms, and reads happen far more often than posts.

A new post takes 40 seconds to appear for some followers. Is that a bug?

No. Fan-out runs in background workers, and the requirements allow up to 1 minute of staleness. That allowance is what lets posting return immediately.

Why not fan out a celebrity's post to all 90 million followers?

It's tens of millions of writes for one post, it delays the post for everyone, and it clogs the workers for other users. Instead the celebrity is flagged, skipped by workers, and merged in at read time.

Why doesn't sharding the post cache fix a viral post?

Sharding by post ID puts all reads of that one post on one shard, so the hot key just moves from DynamoDB to Redis. Replicas, each able to serve any post, split that one key's load N ways.

Why use a timestamp cursor instead of ?page=2?

New posts keep arriving at the top. With offsets, everything shifts and you see duplicates. "Posts older than 10:12" always means the same set, no matter what arrived since.

Why is following someone a PUT?

Following the same person twice should leave the same state. PUT signals an idempotent operation, so a retried request after a network blip is harmless.
