How a 30-minute delivery app knows what's in stock near you

By Asfak Shahrier

Track System designLevel Case studyStatus GrowingAbout 14 min read

I've built checkout flows twice, a cart in an Angular dealership app and a Stripe checkout in Laravel, and both times "is it in stock?" was a single column I barely thought about. Quick commerce apps like Gopuff can't be that relaxed: stock is spread across thousands of small warehouses, and only the ones a driver can reach within an hour count. Two people tapping "Order" on the last bag of chips at the same moment must not both win. These notes rebuild that system step by step.

Before we start

How this case study is built

Five versions, each fixing what the last one couldn't handle.

v1One warehouse, one inventory table
v2Many warehouses: add up stock from the nearby ones
v3Orders and stock change in one transaction
v4"Nearby" means drive time, not a straight line
v520K reads/s: cache, replicas, region partitions
Why this order

WhatsApp was about moving messages and LeetCode about running strangers' code. This one is about a number that must be right: how many of an item are left. Reads can be a little stale (nobody minds seeing "3 left" when there are really 2), but the write that takes the last unit must be exact. Most of the design follows from treating those two paths differently.

Step 1

What the system must do

Functional

  • Given my location, show which items can reach me within 1 hour
  • Availability is the total across all nearby warehouses
  • Order several items in one order

Non-functional

  • Availability answers in under 100 ms (it feels like search)
  • Orders are strongly consistent: no item sold twice
  • 10,000 warehouses, 100,000 catalog items
  • About 10 million orders a day

Left out on purpose

  • Payments
  • Drivers, routing and the delivery itself
  • Search and catalog APIs
  • Cancellations and returns
  • Security and disaster recovery

Why it works this way

Step 3

The full architecture at a glance

The map we're building toward. Two services, one shared helper, one database. Each later section zooms into one part.

Why it works this way

Inventory and orders live in the same Postgres database on purpose. That is what lets one transaction change both. The price is that they scale together, but at 10 million orders a day (about 100 per second) a partitioned Postgres handles the writes fine. The heavy load is reads, and those are pushed away from the leader.

Step 4

What happens when you open the app

Follow the numbers. The trick is that "what's available" is never stored anywhere ready-made: it's computed from your location each time (and then cached).

Why it works this way

The customer doesn't care which warehouse their chips come from. If DC A has 2 and DC B has 3, and both can reach you in time, the app shows 5. That union across nearby DCs is the core idea. In a real product the catalog (names, photos, keyword search) would move to its own search index such as Elasticsearch, and this service would only add up counts.

Deep dive 1

Never selling the same bag of chips twice

Strong consistency here means: if one unit is left, exactly one of the two people racing for it gets it. Here's how the naive version breaks.

BAD

Read, then write

Check the count in one query and update in another, with nothing in between. Fine in testing, broken under real traffic, as shown above. (This one is my own starting point, not from the source.)

GOOD

Two databases + distributed lock

Orders and inventory live in separate stores. Lock the items, create the order, decrement stock, unlock. But a crash between "create order" and "decrement" leaves the two out of sync, needing cleanup sweeps. And A locks chips then wants soda while B holds soda and wants chips: deadlock.

GREAT

One Postgres transaction

Keep orders and inventory in the same database. Check stock, mark it ordered, insert the order rows and commit, all as one unit. If any item is short, the whole thing rolls back. Chosen.

Why it works this way

This is the same tool from my transactions and row-lock notes, just with higher stakes. In Laravel it would be DB::transaction() with lockForUpdate() on the inventory rows. One thing the PostgreSQL docs made clear to me: under SERIALIZABLE, a conflict surfaces as error code 40001, Postgres never retries it for you, and the retry must rerun the whole transaction, including the stock check, not just the failed statement. The trade-off of all-or-nothing: one missing item fails the whole order. That's better than a half-filled order the customer didn't agree to; the app can show "soda just sold out" and let them resubmit.

Deep dive 2

Which warehouses can reach me in an hour?

A warehouse 10 km away across a river with one bridge can be slower than one 25 km away on a highway. Straight-line distance lies, but asking a maps API about every warehouse is far too slow and costly.

BAD

Distance in SQL

Store each DC's lat/long and filter with a straight-line (Haversine) distance. Fast, but blind to roads, rivers, borders and traffic.

BAD

Travel-time API for every DC

Accurate, but you'd ask about 10,000 warehouses per request when almost all of them are hours away. Too many external calls.

GREAT

Prune first, then ask

Keep DCs in memory (re-synced every ~5 minutes). Drop everything outside ~60 miles, the most optimistic distance an hour of driving could cover. Send only the survivors to the travel-time API. Chosen.

Why it works this way

10,000 coordinates fit easily in memory, so the radius check is a quick loop with no database call (my estimate: a few hundred KB). If the DC list grew much bigger or lived only in the database, PostGIS does the same pre-filter in SQL: ST_DWithin(dc.geog, me, 96560) uses meters for the geography type (60 miles ≈ 96,560 m) and its built-in bounding-box check can use a spatial index, so it never scans every row. It's the geospatial cousin of the "cheap check first" idea from my rate limiter notes.

Deep dive 3

Answering 20,000 availability checks a second

People browse a lot more than they buy. A quick estimate turns "10 million orders a day" into the read load we actually face.

BAD

Every read hits the leader

The same database that must commit orders exactly would also absorb 20K summing queries a second. Reads would slow down writes.

GREAT

Redis cache in front

Cache each result for about a minute. On a miss, query and store. The Orders Service deletes the affected keys after it changes stock.

GREAT

Read replicas

Availability reads go to replicas, which can lag slightly; orders always go to the leader. Add replicas as traffic grows. Both chosen, together.

Why it works this way

A slightly wrong read is cheap: at worst the app shows an item that's just sold out, and the order transaction refuses it with a clear message. A wrong write is expensive. So reads get speed (cache, replicas, staleness allowed) and writes get correctness (leader, transaction). The order path never trusts the cache or a replica.

Deep dive 4

Splitting inventory by region

A shopper in New York never needs stock from a warehouse in Los Angeles. So there's no reason for their query to look at it.

OptionVerdictWhy
One big inventory tableBADEvery query and index covers the whole country
Partition by region + replicasGREATQueries stay small; replicas spread the read load. Chosen.
Why it works this way

The partition key matches how people query: by where they are. The ongoing work is balancing: a dense city region gets far more traffic than a rural one, so replica counts and partition sizes need watching. In Bangladesh I'd reach for a district or postcode prefix instead of a US zip, but the idea is the same.

Reflection

What surprised me

The boring answer won

I expected Redis locks or some clever queue for stock. The best answer was one plain Postgres transaction, the same thing I use in Laravel every week.

Stale is fine, sometimes

The availability page is allowed to be a minute old. Correctness only matters at the moment money and stock change hands.

Straight lines lie

In a city split by rivers and traffic jams, "nearest warehouse" by distance can be the wrong one. Drive time is the only honest measure.

Practice

Try it yourself

Think each one through (or explain it to a friend), then open the answer.

DC A has 2 bags of chips, DC B has 3, DC C has 10. A and B can reach you in 40 minutes, C needs 80. What does the app show?

5. Only DCs within the 1-hour drive count, and their stock is added together. DC C is filtered out by the Nearby Service.

Why not just call the travel-time API for every warehouse?

With 10,000 DCs that's 10,000 external calls per request, almost all for warehouses hours away. A cheap ~60-mile radius check removes those first, so only a few candidates reach the API.

Two people order the last unit at the same moment. Walk through what happens.

Both start a transaction on the leader. Postgres detects the conflict: one commits, the other gets a serialization error (40001). The service retries that whole transaction, the stock check now sees 0, and the order fails with "out of stock".

What goes wrong with a distributed lock over two separate databases?

A crash after creating the order but before decrementing stock leaves them out of sync, needing cleanup jobs. And two orders locking items in different orders can deadlock each other.

The cache says 3 left, but there's really 0. Is that a bug?

Not a serious one. The read path is allowed to be slightly stale (TTL ~1 min, plus invalidation). The order transaction checks the real stock on the leader and refuses the order cleanly.

Where does the ~20,000 queries/second number come from?

10M orders ÷ ~100K seconds a day ≈ 100 orders/s. With about 10 page views per visit and 5% of visits ending in a purchase, that's 100 × 10 ÷ 0.05 ≈ 20,000 availability queries per second.
