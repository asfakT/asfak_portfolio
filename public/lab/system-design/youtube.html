How YouTube uploads and streams video

By Asfak Shahrier

Track System designLevel Case studyStatus GrowingAbout 25 min read

A creator uploads a 20 GB video from a shaky connection, and minutes later someone on a slow phone network watches it without buffering. Two simple actions, upload and watch, turn into one of the hardest file problems on the internet. I rebuilt the design one problem at a time, and these are my notes, with a diagram for every step.

Before we start

How this case study is built

The design grows in five versions. Each one fixes a problem the previous version couldn't handle.

v1Upload the file to our server, store it, play it back
v2Upload straight to S3 with a presigned URL
v3Cut the video into segments in many qualities
v4Player picks quality per segment (adaptive streaming)
v5Resumable uploads, queues, cache and CDN at scale
Why this order

In WhatsApp the hard part was messages moving between servers. Here the hard part is the size of the data. Almost every decision in this design comes from one question: how do we keep big files away from places that can't handle them?

Step 1

What the system must do

Functional

  • Creators can upload videos
  • Viewers can watch (stream) videos

Non-functional

  • Highly available (availability over consistency)
  • Videos of tens of GB
  • Low-latency playback, even on slow networks
  • ~1M uploads and ~100M views per day
  • Uploads can resume after a failure

Left out on purpose

  • View counts, comments, search
  • Recommendations
  • Channels and subscriptions
  • Content moderation, bots, monitoring
Why it works this way

The functional list is tiny: upload and watch. The non-functional list is where all the work hides. "Tens of GB" and "slow networks" are what turn a weekend project into YouTube. Choosing availability over consistency is fine here: if a new video's title takes a few seconds to appear everywhere, nobody is harmed.

Step 2

Four words to know first

I couldn't follow the design until these four terms clicked.

Codec

The method that compresses and decompresses video, like H.264, H.265, VP9 or AV1. Each trades compression time, device support, file size and quality.

Container

The file format that holds the video, audio and metadata together, like MP4. Not every device supports every container.

Bitrate

How many bits are sent per second of video. Higher resolution and frame rate mean a higher bitrate, so a slow network needs a lower one.

Manifest

A small text file that tells the player what exists. A primary manifest lists every quality; each media manifest lists the few-second segments for one quality.

Step 3

Entities and API

Entities: User, Video (the actual file and its segments), and VideoMetadata (title, uploader, status, and URLs to the manifests).

EndpointSendsReturns
POST /presigned_urlVideo metadata only (title, size, ...)A presigned URL to upload straight to S3
PATCH /videos/{id}/chunksChunk part number + ETag from S3Updated chunk status
GET /videos/{id}NothingVideoMetadata, including the primary manifest URL
Why it works this way

My first API was POST /upload with the video in the body. It looks natural, but it sends 20 GB through our own servers, which then pass it to storage anyway. Changing it to POST /presigned_url means our server only signs a permission slip; the bytes go directly from the creator to S3.

Step 5

Uploading a video

Two decisions: where the bytes go, and what we store once they arrive.

BAD

Store the original file only

Phones, TVs and browsers need different formats. One raw file plays badly or not at all on many devices.

GOOD

Transcode into several formats

Every device gets a file it can play, but each is still one huge file. You can't start watching before it downloads.

GREAT

Segments in every format

Cut the video into few-second pieces, then make each piece in every quality. Streaming becomes possible, at the cost of a bigger pipeline.

Why it works this way

Metadata lives in Cassandra, partitioned by videoId. Almost every read is "give me video X", a simple key lookup, and Cassandra stays available even when some nodes fail. At ~1M uploads a day we add about 365M rows a year, which a partitioned store handles comfortably.

Step 6

Turning one upload into streamable pieces

After upload, a pipeline turns the original file into segments in every quality, plus the manifests that describe them.

Why it works this way

A 2-hour video transcoded on one machine could take hours. Cut into thousands of segments, it can be spread across many workers and finish far faster. A queue in front of the workers absorbs upload spikes, and the queue's length can tell the system when to add more workers.

Step 7

Watching a video

BAD

Download the whole file

A 10 GB video takes 13+ minutes at 100 Mbps before playing, and a dropped connection loses the progress.

GOOD

Download segments in order

Playback starts after the first few seconds arrive. But the quality is fixed, so a slower network means buffering.

GREAT

Adaptive bitrate streaming

For every segment, the player picks the quality the network can handle right now. Quality drops instead of the video freezing.

Why it works this way

All the intelligence lives in the player, not the server. The server just stores files: manifests and segments. That is why segments can be served from a CDN like any static file, and why the backend barely notices 100M views a day.

Deep dive 1

Resuming an upload after the connection drops

A creator is 90% through a 20 GB upload and the Wi-Fi drops. Starting over would be painful. The fix is to upload in small chunks and remember which ones made it.

Why it works this way

In practice, S3's own multipart upload does most of this work for you. The useful part to understand is the idea: break a big, fragile job into many small jobs whose progress you record. The same idea shows up in database migrations, batch jobs and file sync tools like Dropbox.

Deep dive 2

Staying fast at 100M views a day

PartWhat could breakFix
Video ServiceToo many requests for one serverStateless, so add servers behind the load balancer
CassandraA viral video overloads the one node holding its row (hot partition)Replicate hot rows to more nodes; put an LRU cache by videoId in front
ProcessingUpload spikes overwhelm workersA queue absorbs the burst; queue length triggers autoscaling
S3A bucket lives in one region, so far-away viewers wait longerCross-region replication or a CDN
PlaybackEvery view hitting S3 is slow and expensiveCDN caches popular segments and manifests close to viewers
Why it works this way

Views follow a long tail: a few videos get most of the traffic. That is exactly what caches and CDNs are good at. Popular segments stay at the edge, so a viral video's millions of views barely reach our backend at all.

Beyond the basics

Going further

Ideas I noted for later. Each could be a whole design on its own.

Start processing during upload

The client cuts segments itself and the backend transcodes them as they arrive. Faster, but if the upload is abandoned you're left with half-processed "garbage" segments to clean up.

Resume where I stopped watching

Store the last position per user per video, and send it with the metadata. Simple idea, lots of writes, so it needs batching.

View counts

Exact counts at 100M views a day are expensive. Approximate counters that sync periodically are usually good enough, and the choice is a design of its own.

Reflection

What surprised me

The server never sees the video

I assumed uploads go to "the YouTube server". The app server only hands out permission; the bytes go straight to storage.

The player is the smart part

Quality switching happens on my phone, not on the server. The backend mostly stores small files and serves them like a static website.

It's the same idea as WhatsApp media

Presigned URLs and direct uploads came up in the WhatsApp design too. Seeing a pattern repeat in a second system is when it really stuck.

Practice

Try it yourself

Think each one through (or explain it to a friend), then open the answer.

Why not send the video through the Video Service with POST /upload?

Every byte would pass through our servers and then to storage anyway. That doubles the transfer, ties up servers for minutes per upload, and makes them the bottleneck. A presigned URL lets the client upload directly to S3, which is built for exactly this.

Why cut videos into segments instead of making one file per quality?

Segments let the player start after a few seconds, download only what it needs, and change quality between segments. One big file per quality can't switch mid-video and must be downloaded in order.

The upload fails at chunk 8 of 12. What happens next?

The client reconnects, fetches VideoMetadata, sees chunks 1 to 7 marked Uploaded, and uploads only 8 to 12. Then it calls CompleteMultipartUpload, which triggers processing.

A video goes viral. Which part fails first, and how do you protect it?

The Cassandra node holding that video's metadata becomes a hot partition. Put an LRU cache in front (most reads hit the cache), replicate the hot row to more nodes, and serve segments from the CDN so playback never reaches the origin.

Why is transcoding modeled as a DAG?

Some steps depend on others (manifests need all transcoded segments), but many are independent (each segment can be transcoded on its own). A DAG captures both, so the orchestrator can run independent steps in parallel and retry only the failed ones.

Why choose availability over consistency here?

If a new video's metadata takes a few seconds to appear in every region, nobody is harmed. If the site is down, everyone is. So an always-available store like Cassandra fits better than strict consistency.
